
**Written:** July 2026  
**Context:** Reflection after noticing that my learning has been consistent on paper but slower in actual hands-on progress.

---

## What's Actually Happening

### 1. I avoid starting when I'm not fully sure

When I run into something unfamiliar, my first reaction is usually to pause.

My pattern looks like this:

- "I don't fully understand this yet."
- → I delay trying it.
- → I try to "prepare first."

But I never actually reach a point where I feel fully ready.

That point doesn't exist in practice.

Every time I think I'm getting closer, I simply move the finish line further away.

---

### 2. I confuse preparation with progress

Sometimes I feel productive simply because I'm researching, asking AI questions, or taking notes.

It feels like progress, but I'm not actually interacting with the system.

Instead of:

- trying something
- breaking it
- fixing it

I end up:

- reading about it
- organizing it
- documenting it

The dangerous part is that this *feels* productive.

My brain receives the satisfaction of making progress without paying the cost of uncertainty, mistakes, or debugging.

---

### 3. I try to finish prerequisites that don't actually finish

I keep telling myself:

> "I should finish X before I do Y."

Examples:

- Linux processes before logging
- permissions before exploitation
- networking before building a homelab
- Linux logging before setting up a SIEM

But these subjects aren't separate chapters.

They're interconnected.

None of them are ever truly "finished."

Trying to complete every prerequisite first creates an endless loop where preparation replaces practice.

---

### 4. My notes aren't always coming from experience

A lot of my notes come from:

- reading documentation
- AI explanations
- tutorials
- structured summaries

Instead of situations where I actually needed the concept.

Because of that, I end up with:

- organized knowledge
- clean documentation
- weak intuition

I remember what something **is**.

I don't always remember how it **behaves**.

That difference matters.

---

### 5. Things feel shallow without a real problem

Sometimes I understand a concept intellectually, but it still feels... shallow.

That usually means I encountered the concept before I had a reason to care about it.

For example:

I can write pages about SUID.

But until I actually exploit a vulnerable SUID binary, troubleshoot one, or discover one during enumeration, those notes remain mostly theoretical.

The depth doesn't come from the command.

It comes from the problem that required the command.

---

## The Pattern I Keep Repeating

I've been optimizing for **feeling prepared**.

Instead of interacting with systems while I'm uncertain...

I try to eliminate uncertainty first.

But security doesn't work like that.

The uncertainty never disappears.

I simply become more comfortable operating inside it.

Readiness isn't something I achieve before acting.

It's something that slowly emerges because I keep acting anyway.

---

## What Learning Actually Looks Like

The cycle isn't:

> Read → Take notes → Feel ready → Maybe try

It's:

1. Attempt something.
2. Get stuck.
3. Observe what happened.
4. Form a hypothesis.
5. Read only what's missing.
6. Try again.
7. Repeat.

The understanding comes after the failure—not before it.

---

## What I Think Notes Actually Are

I used to treat notes like something I had to finish before I could begin.

Now I think they're something different.

Notes are:

- compressed experience
- reminders of discoveries
- references to things I've already wrestled with

They're not a substitute for experience.

If I haven't seen something happen with my own eyes...

If I haven't broken it...

If I haven't debugged it...

Then my notes are mostly theoretical.

---

## Naming the Trap

While reflecting on this, I realized there's already a name for part of what I've been doing.

People often call it:

- the **Collector's Fallacy**
- the **Note-Taking Trap**

The idea that collecting, organizing, highlighting, and storing information somehow becomes learning.

I definitely fall into that.

But I think my version is even more specific.

> **Premature system design bias applied to learning.**

I keep designing the structure of knowledge before that knowledge has been tested in reality.

I build the library before going on the expedition.

I organize the map before walking the terrain.

Instead of letting experience shape the notes...

I try to let the notes shape the experience.

That's backwards.

---

## What This Changes

The solution isn't fewer notes.

The solution is changing **when** the notes become detailed.

Instead of documenting every concept immediately...

I should let experience decide what deserves documentation.

Rather than asking:

> "Where should this note go?"

I should first ask:

> "Have I actually used this yet?"

If the answer is "not really," then a simple reference is enough.

Deep notes should come after repeated interaction.

Not before it.

---

## Replace Organization With Retesting

I've noticed something about myself.

Whenever I feel the urge to reorganize my vault...

What I probably need isn't Obsidian.

It's a terminal.

For example:

Instead of reorganizing Linux Processes notes...

I should:

- spawn processes
- inspect them
- kill them
- change priorities
- monitor resource usage
- observe logs
- intentionally break something
- figure out why it behaved that way

Then write notes from *that*.

Experience should shape the notes.

Not the other way around.

---

## A Rule Worth Following

A note deserves a permanent place in my vault when it comes from one of these:

- I broke something.
- Something surprised me.
- I observed the same behavior multiple times.
- I solved a problem I probably won't remember next time.
- I connected multiple concepts through actual experience.

Everything else is probably just reference material.

Useful.

But not yet knowledge I've made my own.

---

## What I Should Do When I Feel Stuck

Whenever I hear myself thinking:

> "I should study X before trying Y."

I should stop.

Then ask:

> "What actually breaks if I try Y right now?"

If the answer is:

**"Nothing important."**

Then I should try it immediately.

If something does break...

That's exactly why I built a VM.

The lab exists so I can be wrong safely.

---

## How I Want To Operate

Going forward:

- Attempt first.
- Accept confusion.
- Observe carefully.
- Form a hypothesis.
- Read with purpose.
- Retry immediately.
- Document what the experience taught me.
- Repeat.

Not every concept needs to become a polished note.

Not every command deserves its own page.

>Experience comes first.
>The notes follow.

---

## Final Reminder

My problem was never that I took too many notes.

My problem was trying to organize knowledge before I had enough experience for that organization to mean anything.

Readiness is not something that arrives before action.

It is something built through repeated action under uncertainty.

The door is already open.

I don't need another map.

I need to walk through it.
